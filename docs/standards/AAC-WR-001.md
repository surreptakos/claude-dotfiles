# How to use this standard

This standard establishes Active Alarm Company's house rules for business writing and document layout. Use The Gregg Reference Manual as the fallback reference under Section 2. House selections, implementation rules, and the reasons for recurring choices are recorded in Appendix F.

The rules apply to company-authored business communication. They do not override legal language, customer-mandated forms, manufacturer nomenclature, engineering conventions, statutory text, executed contracts, or requirements imposed by an authority having jurisdiction.

For an issue not addressed here, follow the order of authority in Section 2. Recurring editorial decisions should be added to the standard through the controlled revision process.

Use a document-specific standard or template only within its approved scope. Performance-review methods, coaching-email forms, and review-cell constraints do not become company-wide rules through this standard. See Rule 2 for the performance-review exception.

# Part I - Governing rules

## 1. Purpose

This standard establishes Active Alarm Company's rules for written communication and document formatting. It applies to internal and external business writing, including email, Teams, letters, memoranda, proposals, scopes of work, reports, SOPs, policies, instructions, meeting notes, customer notices, forms, tables, schedules, technical narratives, contract-related correspondence, Word documents, and PDFs.

The goal is consistency, clarity, accuracy, and ease of use. A reader should be able to identify the point, determine what matters, understand who owns the next action, and find supporting detail without interpreting the writer's intent.

## 2. Order of authority

When two standards conflict, apply them in this order:

1. Applicable law, regulation, code, court requirement, or government form.

2. Executed contract or binding customer requirement.

3. Authority Having Jurisdiction requirement.

4. Manufacturer-required terminology, trademarks, model numbers, or technical nomenclature.

5. Approved document-specific standards and templates, within their stated scope.

6. This standard.

7. The Gregg Reference Manual.

8. Merriam-Webster for U.S. spelling and word treatment.

Do not change contract language, statutory language, quotations, manufacturer terminology, model numbers, or customer-mandated language solely to conform to this standard.

A document-specific standard or template may override a house rule only through an explicit, approved exception that identifies its scope. Its owner must record the exception in the decision log. Other provisions of this standard continue to apply; a template does not authorize changes to legal obligations or technical requirements.

As an exception, performance review documents, their audits, and coaching emails about them follow the AAC performance review standards (SEER, Sum-Ex, Core Message form, third person, one example per item, and minimal commas inside review cells) and “AAC House Layout Standard (Gregg-based).” Where these documents conflict with WR-001, they control for that material. If the two specialized standards conflict, the AAC performance review standards control.

This exception includes Rule 16 (serial comma), Rule 37 (headings and titles), Rule 3’s treatment of “should,” and Rule 66 (e.g. and i.e.), to the extent they conflict with the specialized standards. Apply review-cell requirements only inside review cells and other requirements within their stated scope. All other WR-001 requirements remain in force. The higher authorities listed above still control.

## 3. Mandatory terms used in this standard

**Must** means required. **Should** means the stated treatment is preferred unless a specific reason supports another treatment. **May** means permitted. **Do not** means prohibited unless another authority listed in Section 2 requires it.

# Part II - House writing style

## 4. General writing standard

Company writing must be direct, specific, concise, grammatically complete, appropriate for the intended reader, clear about ownership, clear about required action, clear about deadlines, and accurate about the degree of certainty.

Prefer ordinary business language over inflated, legalistic, or corporate language.

Preferred: Chris will complete the estimate by Friday.

Avoid: It is anticipated that the estimate should hopefully be completed by the end of the week.

State known facts as facts. State estimates as estimates. State assumptions as assumptions.

## 5. State the main point early

For decision-oriented or action-oriented writing, state the principal point early, before supporting detail. A short courtesy opening, such as a thank-you or an introduction to a new contact, may come first. An email requesting a decision should identify the decision or action needed, the person responsible, the deadline if one exists, and the essential reason or context.

Long emails should use a bottom-line-first opening.

Delegation emails are an exception. When an email delegates a task the recipient must carry out live, such as a customer call or a negotiation, state the goal and the concern behind the request before the instruction. The recipient needs the reason to handle what the script does not cover.

Preferred: Please approve the attached $38,500 proposal by Thursday at 3 p.m. so we can release the equipment order Friday.

Do not make the reader search through history to determine why the message was sent.

## 6. Identify the actor

Use a named person, role, company, or party when responsibility matters.

Preferred: Mark will send the revised proposal.

Preferred: The electrical contractor will provide the 120 V circuit.

Preferred: Active Alarm will furnish the card readers.

Avoid: The revised proposal will be sent.

Avoid: Power to be provided by others.

Use passive voice when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or intentionally omitted, but do not use passive voice to hide responsibility.

## 7. Assign actions precisely

A business instruction should answer as many of these questions as necessary: who, what, where, when, and under what condition.

Preferred: Tina: Do not schedule installation until Adam signs the proposal.

Avoid: Let's hold off on scheduling for now.

Do not use **we** when one person owns the action.

## 8. Match certainty to the evidence

Use **is** or **will** for established facts or firm commitments; **should** for a recommendation or expected result; **may, might,** or **could** for genuine uncertainty; and **estimate** or **approximately** when a value is estimated.

Use wording such as **appears** or **based on the information available** when the evidence is incomplete. Do not add qualifiers automatically.

## 9. Plain language

Prefer the shortest familiar word that preserves the intended meaning. Prefer **use** to **utilize**, **before** to **prior to**, **after** to **subsequent to**, **start** to **commence** in ordinary prose, and **because** to **due to the fact that**.

Technical and legal terms are appropriate when they carry a specific technical or legal meaning.

## 10. Avoid filler

Delete phrases that add no information, including routine openings such as **I am writing to inform you that**, **Please be advised that**, **It should be noted that**, **As you are aware**, **At this time**, **In order to**, and **With regard to**.

Preferred: The inspection is scheduled for September 15.

Avoid: Please be advised that the inspection is currently scheduled to take place on September 15.

## 11. Paragraphs

Keep paragraphs focused on one subject. Use short to moderate paragraphs, do not indent the first line, and break long blocks when the subject changes. In Word, create paragraph separation through the style settings in Section 80. In plain-text email or chat, use one blank line. Do not add a blank paragraph on top of paragraph-after spacing.

Do not create a separate paragraph for every sentence without a reason.

Let the thought determine paragraph length. A single-sentence paragraph is appropriate for a distinct decision or instruction; do not impose a line-count rule.

## 12. Sentence length

Use the length required to express the idea clearly. Avoid long strings of independent ideas joined by commas, multiple qualifications buried in the middle of a sentence, and repeated parenthetical interruptions. When a sentence contains several independent requirements, consider a list.

## 13. Contractions

Contractions are acceptable in email, Teams, routine internal communication, and conversational customer communication. Avoid contractions in contracts, formal policies, formal demand letters, certifications, legal notices, and documents where greater formality serves a purpose.

## 14. Requirements, commitments, and permission

Use **must** for a requirement, **will** for a future action or company commitment, **should** for a recommendation, and **may** for permission or legitimate possibility.

Use **shall** primarily in contracts or when preserving established legal language. Do not use **shall** as a more formal substitute for **will** or **must** in ordinary company writing.

# Part III - Punctuation

## 15. Sentence spacing

Use one space after periods, question marks, exclamation points, colons, and semicolons. Do not use two spaces between sentences.

## 16. Serial comma

Use the serial, or Oxford, comma before the final conjunction in a series of three or more items.

intrusion, access control, and video surveillance

furnish, install, program, and test

Use the comma even when the sentence would probably remain understandable without it.

## 17. Commas between independent clauses

Use a comma before **and, but, or, nor, for, so,** or **yet** when the conjunction joins two independent clauses.

The equipment has arrived, but the permit has not been approved.

A comma is normally unnecessary when the conjunction joins only words or phrases.

## 18. Introductory elements

Use a comma after an introductory clause when needed for grammatical separation or clarity.

After the inspection is complete, send the report to the customer.

Short introductory phrases do not require mechanical comma insertion when no ambiguity results.

## 19. Nonessential information

Set off nonessential information with commas.

The revised proposal, which Mark approved yesterday, is attached.

Do not use commas around information necessary to identify the subject.

The proposal that Mark approved yesterday is attached.

## 20. Semicolons

Use a semicolon primarily between closely related independent clauses when no conjunction is used, or between items in a complicated series containing internal commas. Do not use semicolons merely to make writing appear formal. When a period works better, use a period.

## 21. Colons

Use a colon after a complete statement to introduce a list, explanation, quotation, or formal subdivision.

Correct: The project has three remaining items: programming, testing, and training.

Avoid: The remaining items are: programming, testing, and training.

Do not place a colon directly after a verb or preposition unless another rule requires it. Use a colon after the salutation in a formal business letter.

## 22. Hyphens

Use hyphens when words work together as a compound modifier before a noun and the hyphen prevents ambiguity.

60-month agreement

four-door panel

10-year warranty

customer-provided equipment

Do not automatically hyphenate the same expression after the noun. Follow dictionary treatment for established compounds.

Do not hyphenate a compound beginning with an -ly adverb: highly confidential document, publicly traded company. Preserve established dictionary forms; do not remove a permanent hyphen solely because a compound follows the noun. Keep proper-name modifiers intact, as in Lake Zurich office. Refer unresolved compound forms to Section 63.

## 23. Dashes

Use an en dash for ranges in formal documents: pages 4–8, 2025–2026, 8–10 a.m. Do not use an en dash after from or between.

Correct: from 8 a.m. to 10 a.m.

Correct: between Monday and Wednesday

Treat the em dash as a rhythm crutch. Cut it by default and use a comma, colon, parentheses, or a separate sentence instead. In a longer narrative draft, a single em dash may remain where a comma, period, or parenthesis would read worse. Do not use em dashes in contracts, policies, proposals, scopes of work, or any document listed under Rule 13 as requiring formal treatment. A hyphen may replace an en dash in plain-text systems that do not support typographic characters.

## 24. Parentheses

Use parentheses for supplemental information that is useful but secondary.

The panel requires four additional inputs (two per door).

Do not bury essential conditions or actions in parentheses.

## 25. Slashes

Avoid slashes in ordinary prose when the relationship between words can be stated clearly. Prefer **and**, **or**, or **per** to ambiguous constructions such as **and/or**. A slash is acceptable in fractions, approved abbreviations, technical notation, forms, file paths, constrained date fields, and established expressions such as **24/7**.

## 26. Quotation marks

Use quotation marks for exact words, not paraphrases. Periods and commas go inside closing quotation marks in U.S. style. Colons and semicolons go outside. Question marks and exclamation points follow the logic of the quoted material. Do not use quotation marks for emphasis.

## 27. Apostrophes

Use an apostrophe to show possession.

the customer's system

the customers' systems

Chris's estimate

Do not use apostrophes to form ordinary plurals.

Correct: POs, PDFs, 1990s

Avoid: PO's, PDF's, 1990's

## 28. Exclamation points

Do not use exclamation points in ordinary formal business writing. They may be used sparingly in informal internal messages. Never use multiple exclamation points.

## 29. Ellipses

Use ellipses primarily to indicate omitted material in a quotation. Do not use repeated periods to create conversational hesitation in business writing.

# Part IV - Capitalization

## 30. General rule

Capitalize proper names, official organization names, formal titles when used as part of a name, official document titles, formal laws and codes when properly named, and registered product or manufacturer names according to official styling. Lowercase common nouns.

## 31. Company name

Use the official legal styling **Active Alarm Company, Inc.** when the legal entity name is required. Use **Active Alarm Company** or **Active Alarm** when the full legal entity name is unnecessary. Use **AAC** primarily in internal material or after defining the abbreviation when external use is appropriate.

## 32. Job titles

Capitalize a formal title immediately before a person's name when it functions as a title. Lowercase the title when it follows the name or stands alone in ordinary prose.

General Manager Daniel Gatsakos

Daniel Gatsakos, general manager

In organization charts, signature blocks, directory listings, form labels, and table headings, use title case for job titles.

## 33. Departments and functions

Capitalize the official name of a formally designated department or group. Lowercase a generic reference such as **the sales team** or **our accounting function**. Do not capitalize a word merely because it is important.

## 34. Customers, vendors, and parties

Use an organization's preferred spelling, capitalization, spacing, and punctuation. Preserve established names and trademarks. Do not normalize another organization's official name to fit house capitalization.

## 35. Systems and services

Generic system types are lowercase: **fire alarm system, access control system, intrusion alarm system, video surveillance system, monitoring service**. Capitalize an official product, service, program, or proprietary platform name.

## 36. Laws, codes, and standards

Capitalize formal titles such as **National Electrical Code** and **Americans with Disabilities Act**. Use the accepted abbreviation after the full name when appropriate. Established industry standards such as **NFPA 72** may be used without expansion when the intended reader will understand them. Do not guess at code editions.

## 37. Headings and titles

Use sentence case for headings within documents, including Part headings, numbered rules, subheadings, table column headings, form prompts, UI labels, and email headings. Capitalize the first word and any proper names or acronyms. Reserve headline-style capitalization for document titles and official document names. Do not add a period to a standalone heading. Use run-in headings only in legal documents, and end each run-in heading with a colon.

# Part V - Numbers, dates, time, money, and measurement

## 38. House number style

The house figure style spells out numbers from one through ten and uses figures for numbers over ten in ordinary business prose. The exceptions in this Part override the general rule.

three technicians

ten doors

11 cameras

24 customers

## 39. Related numbers

When related numbers appear together and one would require figures, use figures for all related values.

Preferred: The first building has 8 doors, and the second has 14.

Unrelated counts follow their own rules: three account executives closed 14 contracts. Avoid adjacent numeral groups; write two 5-year contracts or recast the sentence. Ordinary nouns such as sites or customers do not, by themselves, create a measurement exception.

## 40. Numbers at the beginning of a sentence

Spell out a number that begins a sentence. Recast the sentence when spelling the number would be awkward.

Better: The inspection identified 127 devices.

## 41. Indefinite numbers

Spell out indefinite numbers and amounts when no precise quantity is intended.

hundreds of devices

a few thousand dollars

Do not turn an indefinite estimate into a false exact figure.

## 42. Dates

Use **September 9, 2026** in narrative text. Do not use ordinal endings when the month comes first. When a month and year appear without a day, do not use a comma: **September 2026**.

Within a sentence, set off the year with commas when a complete month-day-year date requires it.

The agreement signed April 15, 2026, remains in effect.

## 43. Numeric dates

Avoid numeric dates in narrative documents because formats can be misread. Use numeric dates in forms, space-constrained tables, software fields, file names, and system exports. Use **YYYY-MM-DD** where a machine-sortable date is useful.

## 44. Time

Use figures: **8 a.m., 8:30 a.m., 3 p.m., 3:15 p.m.** Use lowercase **a.m.** and **p.m.** with periods in formal prose. Do not use unnecessary zeros. Use **noon** and **midnight** when clearer than 12 a.m. or 12 p.m.

For hard deadlines involving recipients in different zones, include the time zone.

## 45. Time ranges

Use **8–10 a.m.** When the period changes, use **11 a.m.–1 p.m.** With **from**, write **from 8 a.m. to 5 p.m.** Do not combine **from** with a dash range.

## 46. Money

Use figures and the dollar sign for specific monetary amounts: **$5, $42.50, $1,250, $28,882**. Do not add **.00** to whole-dollar amounts in ordinary prose unless uniform decimals serve a financial, contractual, or tabular purpose.

Use combinations such as **$1.6 million** for large rounded amounts when clearer.

In a column that mixes whole-dollar amounts and amounts with cents, use two decimal places for every comparable entry: $12.50, $15.00, $8.25. See Sections 96 and 97 for unit labels and alignment.

## 47. Percentages

In ordinary narrative prose, use figures and spell out **percent**: **39 percent, 5.5 percent**. Use the percent symbol in tables, financial schedules, dashboards, charts, technical calculations, data-heavy management reports, and space-constrained forms.

Do not alternate between **39 percent** and **39%** within the same type of presentation without a reason.

Distinguish percent from percentage points. A change from 17.8 percent to 20 percent is an increase of 2.2 percentage points. Label attainment separately: 118.7 percent of quota describes performance relative to the quota, not a percentage-point change.

## 48. Measurements

Use figures for technical measurements, including values from one through ten: **5 feet, 8 inches, 2 hours, 6 pounds, 3 volts**. In technical documents and tables, standard unit abbreviations may be used: **5 ft, 8 in, 120 V, 20 A**.

Use words for nontechnical or unemphatic references when figures do not need to stand out.

## 49. Dimensions

Use one format consistently within a document. Narrative text may use **4 feet 6 inches**; a technical schedule may use **4 ft 6 in**. Construction drawings may use established drawing notation. Do not convert dimensions supplied by an engineer, architect, manufacturer, or customer unless necessary.

## 50. Fractions and decimals

Spell out simple nontechnical fractions such as **one-half**. Use figures for technical values such as **0.5 A** or **2.5 in**. Use a zero before a decimal less than one.

## 51. Ages, durations, and terms

Use figures when the number carries technical, contractual, or emphatic significance: **a 5-year agreement, a 60-month term, a 2-hour test**. Nontechnical references may follow ordinary figure style.

## 52. Ordinal numbers

Spell out ordinal numbers first through tenth: **first attempt, third floor, tenth anniversary**. Use figures from 11th on. Use figures when the number functions as an identifier, technical reference, or established designation. Do not use ordinal endings with month-first dates.

## 53. Telephone numbers

Use **847-555-1234** (hyphens, no parentheses) for U.S. telephone numbers in ordinary business material. Use the country's established international format when communicating internationally.

# Part VI - Abbreviations and acronyms

## 54. General rule

Do not abbreviate ordinary words merely to save space. Abbreviations are appropriate in tables, forms, technical documents, drawings, schedules, and established business terminology. When in doubt in narrative prose, spell the word out.

## 55. Acronyms

On first use, spell out an unfamiliar term and place the acronym in parentheses: **authority having jurisdiction (AHJ)**. After that, use **AHJ**.

An acronym does not need to be defined when the intended audience can reasonably be expected to know it. Customer-facing material should use customer-friendly terminology rather than unnecessary internal acronyms.

## 56. All-capital abbreviations

Do not normally insert periods in initial-based capital abbreviations: **CEO, PDF, PO, NFPA, RMR**. Use an organization's official style when it differs.

## 57. a.m. and p.m.

Use **a.m.** and **p.m.** in formal prose. Exceptions are permitted in software, calendar interfaces, and systems that impose another display format.

## 58. Corporate suffixes

Preserve the organization's own legal styling. Do not add or remove a comma before **Inc., Corp., LLC,** or **Ltd.** when reproducing a legal company name.

## 59. Personal suffixes and credentials

Respect the individual's established style. Use professional and academic credentials only when relevant to the communication or expected in the document type.

## 60. States

In ordinary narrative prose, spell out state names: **Lake Zurich, Illinois**. In postal addresses and other mailing formats, use USPS abbreviations: **Lake Zurich, IL 60047**.

# Part VII - Spelling and word treatment

## 61. U.S. English

Use standard U.S. spelling: **color, center, organize**. Preserve an organization's own spelling in its proper name.

## 62. Modern technology terms

Use **email, website, internet,** and **online** unless reproducing another organization's official terminology or quoted material.

## 63. Common word pairs

Use the established noun/adjective form and the two-word verb form where applicable: **setup / set up; follow-up / follow up; login / log in; backup / back up**.

Additional house pairs are handoff / hand off, sign-off / sign off, and breakdown / break down. Check Merriam-Webster for established solid, hyphenated, or open forms; do not build a general rule from one example. Preserve exact product names, customer terminology where required, and software field labels. Record a recurring house exception in Appendix F.

## 64. Ampersand

Use **and** in ordinary prose. Use **&** only when it is part of an official name, approved branding, a compact table heading where space requires it, or an established abbreviation.

## 65. etc.

Use **etc.** sparingly. Do not write **and etc.** Do not use **etc.** when omitted items could affect the reader's understanding of a requirement, procedure, or scope.

## 66. e.g. and i.e.

Prefer **for example** and **that is** in ordinary business prose. The abbreviations **e.g.** and **i.e.** are acceptable in parenthetical material and technical writing when used correctly.

# Part VIII - Grammar and usage

## 67. Complete sentences

Use complete sentences in normal prose. Fragments are acceptable in headings, labels, tables, forms, bulleted lists, informal Teams messages, and short action lists. Do not use fragments to create artificial emphasis in formal documents.

## 68. Agreement

Subjects and verbs must agree in number. Pronouns must clearly identify their antecedents. Rewrite a sentence when the reader could reasonably misunderstand who **he, she, they, it, this,** or **that** refers to.

## 69. Singular they

Use singular **they** when a person's gender is unknown, gender is irrelevant, or the individual uses they/them pronouns. Rewrite awkward constructions rather than repeatedly using **he/she**.

## 70. That and which

Use **that** for a clause necessary to identify or restrict the noun. Use **which** for supplemental information, normally with commas.

The panel that failed inspection must be replaced.

The existing panel, which is in the electrical room, will remain.

## 71. Who and that

Use **who** for people in ordinary writing. Use **that** for objects and organizations when appropriate.

## 72. Who and whom

Use **whom** when grammar requires it in formal writing. In ordinary business writing, recast a sentence if the grammatically formal construction sounds unnatural or distracts from the point.

## 73. Parallel construction

Items performing the same grammatical function should use the same structure. Lists of actions should normally begin with parallel verbs.

Inspect the panel.

Test each initiating device.

Document all deficiencies.

## 74. Pronoun reference

Avoid vague uses of **this, that, it,** or **they** when the referenced noun is not obvious. Repeat or replace the noun when doing so removes ambiguity.

# Part IX - Document layout

## 75. Page size

Default to U.S. Letter, **8.5 x 11 inches**, portrait orientation. Use another page size or orientation only when the document type or content requires it.

## 76. Margins

Default margins are **1 inch** on all sides. Approved letterhead and controlled templates may use different top or bottom margins. Do not reduce margins solely to force a document onto fewer pages if readability suffers.

## 77. Default font

Unless an approved template specifies otherwise, use Aptos 11 pt for sustained body text. Use the Word style definitions in Appendix E: document title 20 pt bold; Heading 1 14 pt bold; Heading 2 12 pt bold; Heading 3 11 pt bold; tables 10 pt; footnotes and source notes 9 pt.

Use one font family throughout a routine business document unless another font has a defined purpose.

Use an alternate font only when an approved template specifies it. A software default does not override this standard.

## 78. Text color

Use black or near-black for body text. Approved company brand colors may be used for titles, headings, rules, table headers, and limited visual emphasis. Do not use color as the only way to communicate meaning. Documents must remain understandable when printed in grayscale.

## 79. Alignment

Use left-aligned body text. Do not fully justify ordinary business documents. Center text only when the document design calls for it, such as cover-page titles, certain table headings, or formal certificates.

## 80. Line and paragraph spacing

For Word body text, use 1.08 line spacing, 0 pt before, 6 pt after, and no first-line indent. Use style-based spacing instead of empty paragraphs. Do not combine paragraph-after spacing with blank paragraphs to create the same separation.

Turn off automatic hyphenation in routine business documents. Set paragraph spacing explicitly in each template; do not depend on the application default. Disable contextual spacing (the option to omit space between paragraphs of the same style) where it would suppress the specified spacing. Plain-text email uses one blank line instead of Word paragraph spacing.

## 81. Tabs and alignment

Do not align content by inserting repeated spaces. Use tab stops, tables, indents, and paragraph styles. This is especially important for documents that may later be edited, converted to PDF, or opened on another computer.

## 82. Headings

Headings must describe the content that follows. Do not use a heading without content beneath it. Do not leave a heading alone at the bottom of a page. Use Word's built-in heading styles or approved company styles rather than manually formatting every heading.

Set more space before a heading than after it: Heading 1 and Heading 2 use 12 pt before and 4 pt after; Heading 3 uses 9 pt before and 3 pt after. Enable Keep with next and Keep lines together for heading styles. A heading starts the section it describes; it does not close the previous section.

## 83. Heading hierarchy

Use no more levels than necessary. The controlled-document hierarchy is Part, numbered rule, and subheading. Do not skip levels. Three heading levels are sufficient for most company documents. Number headings when readers need to refer to sections; use an approved style-linked numbering scheme for new documents. Preserve established rule identifiers during routine revisions.

## 84. Bold, italics, underlining, and capitals

Use bold for headings, important labels, and limited emphasis. Use italics for publication titles, limited emphasis, and certain technical or foreign terms. Do not underline text for emphasis; underlining should generally indicate a hyperlink. Avoid ALL CAPITALS for running text.

## 85. Page breaks

Insert an actual page break when a new page is required; do not use repeated hard returns. Enable widow/orphan control so a paragraph split across pages leaves at least two lines on each page. Keep a heading with the following paragraph and a table caption with the first table row. A short paragraph or list may be kept together when doing so does not create a large gap.

Do not leave a closing or signature block alone on a continuation page. Move at least two lines of the final body paragraph with it, or adjust the preceding layout without reducing legibility. Inspect every page after pagination changes; a clean first page does not establish that later pages are correct.

## 86. Headers and footers

Formal documents longer than one page should normally include an abbreviated document or project name when useful, plus page numbering and document identification or revision when controlled-document status matters. Preferred page numbering is **Page 2 of 7**.

Use continuous Arabic page numbering for routine controlled standards and reports. Use a separate preliminary-page scheme only when a document-specific standard requires it; a title page or Roman-numeral front matter is not mandatory merely because a document exceeds five pages.

## 87. First page

Do not repeat an unnecessary running header on the first page when letterhead, a cover page, or the document title already supplies the necessary identification.

## 88. Hyperlinks

Use descriptive hyperlink text when practical rather than **Click here**. In documents likely to be printed, show the URL in a source note or reference section when the destination itself matters.

# Part X - Lists

## 89. When to use a list

Use a list when the reader needs to distinguish discrete actions, requirements, options, conditions, deliverables, findings, or components. Do not turn ordinary connected prose into a list solely to shorten sentences.

Two items may form a list when separate alternatives, responsibilities, or requirements are easier to use that way. A list has no minimum item count beyond what the content requires.

## 90. Bulleted lists

Use bullets when sequence does not matter. Use the same grammatical construction for all items. If each bullet is a complete sentence, capitalize the first word and end each bullet with a period. If bullets are short labels or fragments, periods may be omitted. Use one treatment consistently within the list.

Capitalize each standalone item. If a list mixes sentences and fragments, revise it for parallel construction where practical and apply one end-punctuation treatment to the entire list.

## 91. Numbered lists

Use numbers when order matters, the reader will refer back to individual items, the list contains procedural steps, or priority or sequence matters. Do not number a list if the numbers serve no functional purpose.

Restart numbering at 1 for each independent list. Continue numbering only when the items belong to the same sequence. Use native list numbering rather than typed numbers or spaces; check the restart after inserting, moving, or pasting items.

## 92. Introductory statements

Use a colon when a complete sentence introduces a list. If the list grammatically completes the introductory wording, punctuate the list based on the construction and clarity required.

## 93. Nested lists

Avoid more than three levels. For ordinary lists, use **1., a., i.** For technical controlled documents using decimal numbering, use **1, 1.1, 1.1.1**. Do not mix numbering systems without a reason.

Use a consistent hanging indent. The default first-level marker starts 0.25 inch from the left margin and the text starts at 0.5 inch; move each deeper level another 0.5 inch to the right. Use 0 pt before and 3 pt after ordinary items. Keep a parent item with its first sub-item. An approved template may use different spacing for grouped requirements.

# Part XI - Tables

## 94. Use of tables

Use a table when readers need to compare values across consistent categories. Do not use a table for material that reads more naturally as prose.

## 95. Table titles

Place a short descriptive title above a table and keep it with the table. Number tables when readers need cross-references or the document is formal or controlled; appendix tables may use the appendix letter, as in Table B1. Use sentence case for the title.

For sourced data, put explanatory notes below the table and the source note beneath those notes. Identify the source, reporting period or as-of date, and a worksheet, range, section, or record reference when needed to verify the figures. Use lowercase superscript letters for cell-specific notes and define each letter below the table.

## 96. Column headings

Use concise, sentence-case column headings. State units and scale in the heading when they apply to the entire column, such as Revenue ($), GPM (%), or RMR ($/month). Do not repeat the same unit in each value when the heading already supplies it. Right-align a numeric heading with its values; align text headings with their text unless an approved table format specifies otherwise.

## 97. Numeric alignment

Left-align text and right-align comparable numbers. Align decimals by the decimal point and use consistent decimal places for comparable values unless additional precision carries meaning. Keep dates consistent and either left-aligned or centered. Treat identifiers as text and preserve leading zeros. Do not center financial values or other quantities that readers must compare.

## 98. Table formatting

Use adequate cell padding, intentional alignment, and limited borders. A distinct header row and a rule above totals are appropriate; avoid a full grid unless it helps the reader follow a complex table. Label totals and subtotals. Repeat the header row on each continuation page and keep a row together when it can fit on one page.

Fit the table to the usable page width. Wrap text or adjust column widths first. For a table that remains too wide, use a landscape section, split it at a logical boundary with repeated identifiers, or move it to an appendix. Do not shrink table text below 9 pt to force a fit. Preserve the relationships among data when splitting a table.

## 99. Empty and zero values

Distinguish among 0 for a measured or confirmed zero, N/A for not applicable, Unknown when information should exist but is not known, and Unlogged when activity or a value was not recorded. Use a dash for not applicable only when the table defines that convention. Do not leave an unexplained blank in a completed data table. Empty response or signature fields are permitted in an uncompleted form.

# Part XII - Email

## 100. Subject lines

Every business email should have a meaningful subject. The subject should identify the topic, project or customer when useful, and required action when applicable.

Clearbrook Northwind - Proposal Approval Needed

September Sales Report - Review Before Wednesday

Avoid subjects such as **Question, Update, FYI,** or **Important** without additional context. Update the subject when a long thread changes to a materially different topic.

## 101. Opening

Start with the purpose of the email. Open an internal request to a named colleague with a greeting. In external or transactional email a greeting is optional. Do not routinely open with filler such as **I hope this email finds you well**.

## 102. Long emails

When an email is long, decision-oriented, or contains substantial background, put the bottom line first. The first paragraph should state what is happening, what is needed, who owns it, and by when. Then provide explanation.

## 103. Action ownership

Name the person who owns an action. When several people have separate responsibilities, assign each responsibility separately.

Chris: Complete the estimate.

Tina: Hold scheduling until the proposal is signed.

## 104. Deadlines

Use a specific deadline when timing matters. Prefer **Friday, September 11, at 3 p.m.** to **ASAP**. Use **ASAP** only when immediate action is genuinely required and the timing cannot be stated more accurately. When the sender controls the next step, a sequence may replace a clock deadline: **call him before Lynne sends the letter**.

## 105. To and Cc

Put in **To** the people expected to act, directly responsible, or directly addressed. Put in **Cc** people who need visibility or a record but do not own the requested action. Do not use Cc as a substitute for telling someone what they need to do.

## 106. Attachments

When an attachment is necessary to the action, identify it. If several attachments exist, identify the important ones by name or purpose. Use filenames that allow the recipient to identify the document after saving it.

Before sending, check whether the attachment is a clean issue or a review copy. Remove comments and tracked changes from a clean issue; preserve them when the recipient is being asked to review those changes. A hidden-markup display is not a clean copy.

## 107. Email closing

Routine internal messages do not require a ceremonial closing. An email that delegates a task ends with how the sender will learn the task is done: **Please let me know when you have called him so we can send the letter.** That line is the loop closure, not a ceremonial closing. Formal external messages may use **Thank you, Regards,** or **Sincerely,** as appropriate. Do not use a closing that conflicts with the substance or tone of the message.

## 108. Email signature

Use the approved company signature format. At minimum include name, job title, company, and primary telephone number; include email or website when useful. Do not add personal quotations, inspirational sayings, unapproved images, decorative fonts, or excessive colors.

# Part XIII - Teams and business chat

## 109. General standard

Teams is less formal than email, but business-critical communication still requires clarity. A message assigning work should identify the person, action, and timing when relevant.

Chris, please send Mark the estimate before the 2 p.m. meeting.

## 110. Mentions

Use @mentions when a person needs to act, needs immediate attention, or is being added to a discussion where input is required. Do not @mention large groups for routine information.

## 111. Permanent records

Do not rely on Teams chat as the only record of a material customer commitment, contract decision, pricing approval, scope change, policy decision, safety issue, or project authorization. Record the final decision in the appropriate permanent system, document, ticket, CRM record, or email when required.

# Part XIV - Business letters

## 112. House letter format

Use full-block format. All elements begin at the left margin except items built into approved letterhead. Do not indent body paragraphs. Use a colon after the salutation and a comma after the complimentary closing.

## 113. Letter sequence

A formal letter should normally contain company letterhead, date, recipient name, recipient title, organization, street address, city/state/ZIP Code, salutation, optional subject line, body, complimentary closing, signature, typed name, title, enclosure or attachment notation when applicable, and copy notation when applicable.

## 114. Inside address

Use the recipient's preferred organization name and address. Spell out ordinary address terms in the inside address when space permits. Use USPS abbreviations where postal conventions call for them.

## 115. Salutation

Use **Dear Mr. Smith:**, **Dear Ms. Johnson:**, or the person's professional title. In an established first-name business relationship, **Dear Mark:** is acceptable. Avoid generic salutations when a specific person or role can reasonably be identified.

## 116. Subject line

A subject line may appear in the approved template before or after the salutation. Use a concise description. Do not use both **Subject:** and **Re:** for the same line.

## 117. Signature block

Leave adequate space for an actual or electronic signature. Follow approved company letterhead and signature standards.

Keep the closing, signature, typed name, and title together. On a continuation page, include at least two lines of body text above the closing, as required by Section 85.

# Part XV - Memoranda

## 118. Memo use

Use a memorandum when information should become part of a formal internal record. Use email or Teams for routine temporary coordination.

## 119. Memo heading

Use the guide words **DATE, TO, FROM, SUBJECT** in the approved memo template. Align the entries following the guide words. The subject should state the specific matter addressed.

## 120. Memo body

Use full-block paragraphs, no paragraph indentation, and the Word body spacing in Section 80. State the purpose in the first paragraph. Use headings when a memo exceeds about one page or addresses several distinct issues.

# Part XVI - Reports

## 121. Report structure

Use the sections appropriate to the report's purpose. Possible components include cover or title page, executive summary, table of contents, purpose or background, findings, analysis, recommendations, action items, appendices, and source notes. Do not add sections solely to make a report appear formal.

## 122. Executive summary

Use an executive summary when a decision-maker needs the conclusion without reading the entire report. State the principal finding, material evidence, recommended decision or action, and major risk or limitation. Do not use the executive summary merely to describe what the report contains.

## 123. Tables of contents

Use a table of contents for longer formal reports, manuals, policies, SOP collections, and documents where readers need to navigate among sections. As a house rule, consider a TOC once a document exceeds approximately five substantive pages. Use automatic heading-based generation in Word.

Keep table-of-contents entries synchronized with the document headings. Align page numbers at a right-hand tab stop with dot leaders. Update the entire table after edits and verify the listed page numbers against the issued document.

## 124. Data dates

Every recurring management report should state the reporting period or data-as-of date. Do not present stale data as current.

## 125. Findings and interpretation

Separate what the data says, why it happened, and what action should follow. Do not state an interpretation as though it were a measured fact.

# Part XVII - Proposals and scopes of work

## 126. Scope writing

Write scope items so the reader can determine who performs the work, what is furnished, what is installed or changed, the quantity, the location, and any relevant condition or assumption.

Preferred: Active Alarm will furnish and install two card readers at the rear double doors.

Avoid: Add readers at rear doors.

## 127. Quantities

State quantities where omission could create a scope dispute. Do not rely on plural nouns alone when the exact number matters.

## 128. Locations

Use specific locations. Avoid vague phrases such as **as needed** or **where required** unless another document or standard objectively defines the locations.

## 129. Work by other parties

Identify the party whenever known.

Preferred: The electrical contractor will provide a dedicated 120 V circuit at the control panel.

Avoid: 120 V by others.

Use **by others** only when the responsible party has not been determined or when an established contract format requires it.

## 130. Exclusions and assumptions

State exclusions and assumptions directly.

Price excludes 120 V power wiring.

Pricing assumes existing conduit is reusable.

Avoid vague catch-all language when a specific exclusion or assumption can be identified.

# Part XVIII - Procedures and SOPs

## 131. Standard SOP structure

Unless the controlled SOP system requires another structure, use: title, document number, version, effective date, owner, purpose, scope, responsibilities, required inputs or prerequisites, procedure, exceptions or escalation, records or system entries, related documents, and revision history.

## 132. Procedure steps

Use numbered steps. Write each primary step as an instruction and start with a verb whenever practical.

1. Open the customer record in Zoho CRM.

2. Confirm the site address.

3. Attach the signed proposal.

## 133. One action per step

A step may contain closely related actions, but do not bury several independent requirements in one paragraph. Use substeps when necessary.

## 134. Roles rather than names

Controlled procedures should assign recurring responsibilities to roles rather than individual employees. Use a person's name only when the document concerns that specific person rather than the continuing role.

## 135. Exceptions

State who can authorize an exception.

Preferred: The General Manager may approve an exception to this requirement in writing.

Avoid: Exceptions may be made when necessary.

## 136. Screenshots

Use screenshots only when visual information materially helps the instruction. Show only relevant screen content, avoid unnecessary personal or customer information, keep the image legible when printed, and use a short caption when the purpose is not obvious. Do not use a screenshot as a substitute for writing the required action.

# Part XIX - Contract and legal writing

## 137. Existing legal language

Do not revise approved contract language solely to make it conform to ordinary business-writing preferences. Legal meaning controls style. Contract forms should have designated owners and version control.

## 138. Defined terms

Capitalize a contractual term only when the document defines it as a term or established legal drafting requires the capitalization. Do not capitalize ordinary nouns merely to make them appear important.

## 139. Shall, must, and will

In company-authored noncontract documents, **must** expresses a requirement, **will** expresses a future action or commitment, and **may** expresses permission. In contracts, preserve approved legal drafting conventions. Do not change **shall** to **will** or **must**, or the reverse, without considering the legal effect.

## 140. Legal names

Use exact legal entity names in contracts, amendments, notices, releases, certificates, legal demands, and other documents where entity identity matters. Do not abbreviate a legal entity name before it has been properly established in the document.

# Part XX - Technical writing

## 141. Technical accuracy controls style

Do not sacrifice technical accuracy for ordinary prose conventions. Preserve manufacturer names, product names, part numbers, model numbers, serial numbers, device identifiers, drawing references, circuit numbers, door numbers, software versions, code sections, and customer identifiers exactly.

## 142. Technical units

Use accepted unit symbols in technical material. Maintain a space between the number and unit when standard technical practice requires it. Follow the applicable engineering or drawing standard when it requires another notation.

## 143. Model and part numbers

Do not insert punctuation, change capitalization, remove leading zeros, convert text to a number, or apply ordinary spelling rules to a model, serial, device, or part identifier. Copy identifiers from the authoritative source whenever practical.

## 144. Code references

When citing a code requirement, identify the source with enough precision for another person to verify it. When known and relevant, include the code or standard, edition, section, and applicable jurisdiction. Do not state that a code requires something unless the requirement has been confirmed.

# Part XXI - File names and version control

## 145. File names

A filename should identify the document without opening it. Recommended structure: **Customer - Project - Document - YYYY-MM-DD**. Add a version when several active drafts must coexist.

Clearbrook - Northwind - Access Control Proposal - 2026-09-09 - v2.docx

## 146. Avoid in file names

Avoid **final, FINAL FINAL, new, newest, updated, copy, use this one**, and random numeric suffixes when a controlled version or date can communicate sequence.

Avoid: Proposal_FINAL_v2_updated.docx

## 147. Final issued documents

A final issued PDF may omit a draft version suffix when a date, proposal number, contract number, or document-control system uniquely identifies it. Do not overwrite executed contracts or other historical records with later revisions.

# Part XXII - Document review

## 148. Writer's review

Review material documents in two passes. Pass 1 checks content and logic. Pass 2 checks language, mechanics, layout, and release readiness. Complete both before sending or publishing.

Before sending or publishing a material document, verify recipient, names, company names, dates, dollar amounts, quantities, percentages, addresses, deadlines, attachment references, technical identifiers, calculations, links, page numbering, version, and required signatures.

Pass 1: Trace each material figure, date, quoted passage, customer name, and technical identifier to its source. Check the reporting period, assumptions, calculations, and whether the conclusion follows. Mark unresolved data as Unknown or Unlogged when appropriate; do not fill a gap with an estimate unless it is labeled as an estimate.

## 149. Read for meaning

Proofreading is not limited to spelling. Confirm that the reader knows why the document was sent, the required action is explicit, the owner and deadline are explicit when needed, assumptions are identified, facts are distinguished from estimates and opinions, and the conclusion follows from the information presented.

Pass 2: Check sentence structure, word treatment, punctuation, number formats, list restarts, headings, table alignment, page numbers, attachments, and version. Update fields and inspect every page of the final layout. Check for isolated headings, closings, signature blocks, clipped text, broken rows, and accidental blank pages.

Give the author short, specific review comments, such as "Verify this quantity against the approved equipment schedule." Use tracked changes or comments for digital review. Before a clean release, resolve those items and remove review markup from the issued copy.

## 150. Automated tools

Spellcheck and grammar-checking software may assist proofreading. They do not control company terminology, contract meaning, technical terminology, manufacturer names, customer names, code references, scope, tone, or business judgment. Do not accept automated edits without review.

# Part XXIII - House format by document type

## 151. Quick format matrix

The matrix in Appendix B establishes the default formatting treatment for routine email, formal letters, memoranda, reports, SOPs, and proposals/scopes. Approved templates control when they intentionally differ from the matrix.

# Part XXIV - Editorial decision rule

## 152. When this standard does not answer the question

Use this decision sequence:

1. Determine whether another controlling document requires a treatment.

2. Determine whether the organization, person, manufacturer, or customer has an official preferred form.

3. Apply this standard when it establishes a house treatment.

4. Apply The Gregg Reference Manual when this standard is silent.

5. Apply Merriam-Webster for spelling or compound treatment when the issue remains unresolved.

6. Select the remaining alternative that communicates the meaning with the least ambiguity.

Once the company makes a recurring editorial decision, add it to this standard through the controlled revision process rather than deciding the same question repeatedly.

# Part XXV - Draft quality and AI tells

## 153. Scope of this part

This part applies to original AAC prose in any deliverable covered by Rule 1. It does not reach contract language, statutory language, quotations, manufacturer terminology, model numbers, or customer-mandated language, which Rule 2 protects.

Apply this part in either of two ways. **Revise** rewrites the draft. **Audit** reports findings without changing the text: name the pattern, quote the line, state the fix. Use audit for specs, contracts, quoted wording, and anywhere a person keeps final say over the words.

## 154. Preserve the writer's voice

Make the minimum effective edit. Keep the writer's cadence, bluntness, humor, stated uncertainty, and useful digressions. This standard sets requirements; it does not impose one voice. Do not flatten distinctive drafts into a single house rhythm.

## 155. Empty adverbs

Extends Rule 10. Delete adverbs that add no information, including **really, just, literally, genuinely, honestly, simply,** and **actually** where the sentence carries the same meaning without them.

Keep an adverb that carries emphasis, records uncertainty, or preserves spoken rhythm. This is not a prohibition on adverbs.

Preferred: The panel failed the test.

Avoid: The panel actually just completely failed the test.

## 156. Faux-insight setups

Do not open with a claim of hidden knowledge. Phrases such as **what nobody tells you, the part everyone misses,** and **here is the thing** promise an insight the sentence then fails to deliver. State the point.

Preferred: Most maglock rejections come from missing AHJ approval, not from the hardware.

Avoid: What nobody tells you about maglocks is that the approval matters more than the hardware.

## 157. Colon-reveal drama

Do not use a colon to stage a reveal. Rule 21 permits a colon after a complete statement introducing a list, explanation, or quotation. It does not license suspense.

Preferred: The system relearns the schedule automatically.

Avoid: The best part: it learns.

## 158. Importance puffery

Do not assert significance in place of stating it. Delete **marks a pivotal moment, stands as a testament, represents a significant step,** and similar. Give the fact and let the reader judge.

## 159. Superficial -ing analysis

Do not append a trailing participial clause that restates the sentence as commentary.

Preferred: The team closed all 14 tickets within the SLA.

Avoid: The team closed all 14 tickets within the SLA, highlighting the team's commitment to quality.

## 160. Weasel attribution

Extends Rule 8. Do not attribute a claim to an unnamed authority. **Experts agree, studies show, it is widely understood,** and **research suggests** are not sources. Name the source or cut the claim. Never invent a source.

## 161. Synonym cycling

Name one actor, system, or tool the same way throughout a document. Rotating synonyms to avoid repetition makes the reader check whether a new thing has been introduced. Rule 143 already governs model and part numbers; this rule extends the same discipline to people, roles, and systems.

## 162. Fake-profound kickers and summary recaps

Do not end on a manufactured resonance, and do not close by restating what the reader has already read. Rule 122 governs executive summaries, which belong at the front. End when the content ends.

## 163. Interpretive metadiscourse

Do not narrate the writing. Delete **the key point is, this distinction matters, it is worth noting,** and **what this means is.** If a point is the key point, state it early under Rule 5.

## 164. Formulaic structures

Avoid the constructions listed in Appendix H: binary contrasts, negative listing, dramatic fragmentation, rhetorical setups, false agency, and the narrator-from-a-distance voice. Rule 6 already requires a named actor; Appendix H covers the remaining patterns.

## 165. Formatting slop

Do not use emoji in headings, decorative bold inside a sentence, or a heading for every paragraph. Parts IX through XI govern headings, lists, and tables in AAC documents and control where they conflict.

## 166. Draft quality check before release

Before release, confirm the draft under Appendix A, then confirm this part: voice preserved, filler and empty adverbs cut, no manufactured insight, every attributed claim sourced, one name per actor, no kicker, no recap. A draft that fails any item returns to the writer.

# Appendix G - Phrase register

Registers the phrase patterns Rules 155 through 163 prohibit. The register is a reference, not an exhaustive list; a phrase absent from it is still subject to the rule it offends. Source recorded in References.

## G1. Throat-Clearing Openers

Remove these announcement phrases. State the content directly.

- "Here's the thing:"
- "Here's what [X]"
- "Here's this [X]"
- "Here's that [X]"
- "Here's why [X]"
- "The uncomfortable truth is"
- "It turns out"
- "The real [X] is"
- "Let me be clear"
- "The truth is,"
- "I'll say it again:"
- "I'm going to be honest"
- "Can we talk about"
- "Here's what I find interesting"
- "Here's the problem though"

Any "here's what/this/that" construction is throat-clearing before the point. Cut it and state the point.

## G2. Emphasis Crutches

These add no meaning. Delete them.

- "Full stop." / "Period."
- "Let that sink in."
- "This matters because"
- "Make no mistake"
- "Here's why that matters"

## G3. Business Jargon

Replace with plain language.

| Avoid | Use instead |
|---|---|
| Navigate (challenges) | Handle, address |
| Unpack (analysis) | Explain, examine |
| Lean into | Accept, embrace |
| Landscape (context) | Situation, field |
| Game-changer | Significant, important |
| Double down | Commit, increase |
| Deep dive | Analysis, examination |
| Take a step back | Reconsider |
| Moving forward | Next, from now |
| Circle back | Return to, revisit |
| On the same page | Aligned, agreed |

## G4. Faux-Insight Setups

Phrases that announce a secret instead of making a claim. Cut the setup. State the content.

- "What nobody tells you"
- "What they don't tell you"
- "The part everyone misses"
- "Here's what most people miss"
- "The missing piece is"
- "Nobody talks about"

## G5. Importance Puffery

Announcing significance instead of naming the change.

- "marks a pivotal moment"
- "stands as a testament"
- "underscores the importance"
- "a shining example of"
- "speaks volumes"
- "a powerful reminder"
- "represents a significant milestone"

## G6. Weasel Attribution

Borrowed authority with no named source. Name the source or cut the claim. Never invent a source to fill the gap.

- "experts agree"
- "studies show"
- "research suggests"
- "scientists say"
- "observers note"
- "industry leaders believe"
- "data indicates" (with no named data)

## G7. Interpretive Metadiscourse

Wrappers that tell the reader how to read the next sentence. Delete the wrapper. Keep the sentence.

- "the key point is"
- "this distinction matters"
- "what this means is"
- "the takeaway is"
- "it's important to understand"
- "this is a crucial distinction"

## G8. Adverbs

Cut empty adverbs. -ly words, softeners, intensifiers, and hedges that add no meaning.

Keep an adverb when it carries real emphasis, uncertainty, or spoken rhythm. "I almost shipped it" is not the same as "I shipped it." "She said it quietly" is not empty.

Empty offenders:

- "really"
- "just"
- "literally"
- "genuinely"
- "honestly"
- "simply"
- "actually"
- "deeply"
- "truly"
- "fundamentally"
- "inherently"
- "inevitably"
- "interestingly"
- "importantly"
- "crucially"

Also cut these filler phrases:

- "At its core"
- "In today's [X]"
- "It's worth noting"
- "At the end of the day"
- "When it comes to"
- "In a world where"
- "The reality is"

## G9. Meta-Commentary

Remove self-referential asides. The essay should move, not announce its own structure.

- "Hint:"
- "Plot twist:" / "Spoiler:"
- "You already know this, but"
- "But that's another post"
- "X is a feature, not a bug"
- "Dressed up as"
- "The rest of this essay explains..."
- "Let me walk you through..."
- "In this section, we'll..."
- "As we'll see..."
- "I want to explore..."

## G10. Performative Emphasis

False intimacy or manufactured sincerity:

- "creeps in"
- "I promise"
- "They exist, I promise"

## G11. Vague Declaratives

Sentences that announce importance without naming the specific thing. Kill these.

- "The reasons are structural"
- "The implications are significant"
- "This is the deepest problem"
- "The stakes are high"
- "The consequences are real"

If a sentence says something is important/deep/structural without showing the specific thing, cut it or replace it with the specific thing.

# Appendix H - Structure register

Registers the structural patterns Rules 156 through 165 prohibit. Read with Rule 6, which governs the named actor, and Parts IX through XI, which control document layout where they conflict.

## H1. Binary Contrasts

These create false drama. State the point directly.

| Pattern | Problem |
|---|---|
| "Not because X. Because Y." / "Not because X, but because Y." | Telegraphed reversal |
| "[X] isn't the problem. [Y] is." | Formulaic reframe |
| "The answer isn't X. It's Y." | Predictable pivot |
| "It feels like X. It's actually Y." | Setup/reveal cliche |
| "The question isn't X. It's Y." | Rhetorical misdirection |
| "Not X. But Y." / "not X, it's Y" / "isn't X, it's Y" | Mechanical contrast |
| "It's not this. It's that." | Same formula, different words |
| "stops being X and starts being Y" | False transformation arc |
| "doesn't mean X, but actually Y" | Negation-then-assertion crutch |
| "is about X but not Y" | False distinction |
| "not just X but also Y" | Additive hedge |

**Instead:** State Y directly. "The problem is Y." "Y matters here." Drop the negation entirely.

## H2. Negative Listing

Listing what something is *not* before revealing what it *is*. A rhetorical striptease.

| Pattern | Problem |
|---|---|
| "Not a X... Not a Y... A Z." | Dramatic buildup through negation |
| "It wasn't X. It wasn't Y. It was Z." | Same structure, past tense |

**Instead:** State Z. The reader doesn't need the runway.

## H3. Dramatic Fragmentation

Sentence fragments for emphasis read as manufactured profundity.

| Pattern | Problem |
|---|---|
| "[Noun]. That's it. That's the [thing]." | Performative simplicity |
| "X. And Y. And Z." | Staccato drama |
| "This unlocks something. [Word]." | Artificial revelation |

**Instead:** Complete sentences. Trust content over presentation.

## H4. Rhetorical Setups

These announce insight rather than deliver it.

| Pattern | Problem |
|---|---|
| "What if [reframe]?" | Socratic posturing |
| "Here's what I mean:" | Redundant preview |
| "Think about it:" | Condescending prompt |
| "And that's okay." | Unnecessary permission |

**Instead:** Make the point. Let readers draw conclusions.

## H5. False Agency

Giving inanimate things human verbs. Complaints don't "become" fixes. Bets don't "live or die." Decisions don't "emerge." A person does something to make those things happen. AI loves this because it avoids naming the actor.

| Pattern | Problem |
|---|---|
| "a complaint becomes a fix" | The complaint did nothing. Someone fixed it. |
| "a bet lives or dies in days" | Bets don't have lifespans. Someone kills the project or ships it. |
| "the decision emerges" | Decisions don't emerge. Someone decides. |
| "the culture shifts" | Cultures don't shift on their own. People change behavior. |
| "the conversation moves toward" | Conversations don't move. Someone steers. |
| "the data tells us" | Data sits there. Someone reads it and draws a conclusion. |
| "the market rewards" | Markets don't reward. Buyers pay for things. |

**Instead:** Name the human. "The team fixed it that week" beats "the complaint becomes a fix." If no specific person fits, use "you" to put the reader in the seat.

## H6. Narrator-from-a-Distance

Floating above the scene instead of putting the reader in it.

| Pattern | Problem |
|---|---|
| "Nobody designed this." | Disembodied observation |
| "This happens because..." | Lecturer voice |
| "This is why..." | Same |
| "People tend to..." | Armchair sociologist |

**Instead:** Put the reader in the room. "You don't sit down one day and decide to..." beats "Nobody designed this."

## H7. Sentence Starters to Avoid

| Pattern | Fix |
|---|---|
| Sentences starting with What, When, Where, Which, Who, Why, How | Restructure. Lead with the subject or the verb. |
| Paragraphs starting with "So" | Start with content |
| Sentences starting with "Look," | Remove |

Wh- openers become a crutch. "What makes this hard is..." becomes "The constraint is..." or better, name the specific constraint.

## H8. Colon-Reveal Drama

A label, a colon, a punchline. Manufactured reveal.

| Pattern | Problem |
|---|---|
| "The best part: it learns." | Colon does the drama. The sentence should. |
| "The kicker: [punchline]" | Same reveal, stock label. |
| "The result: [punchline]" | Outcome announced, then restated. |
| "The truth: [punchline]" | Throat-clearing with a colon. |

**Instead:** One sentence. "It learns from the last run." Save colons for lists and clock times.

## H9. Superficial -ing Analysis

A trailing -ing clause that moralizes the sentence it follows.

| Pattern | Problem |
|---|---|
| "highlighting the team's commitment" | The ship is the commitment. The clause is a caption. |
| "underscoring the importance of" | Importance announced, not shown. |
| "demonstrating a dedication to" | Same caption, new verb. |
| "reflecting a broader trend" | Zooms out to nowhere. |
| "paving the way for" | Fake causality. |

**Instead:** End on the act. "The team shipped on Friday." If the clause names a real consequence, make it a second sentence with an actor.

## H10. Synonym Cycling

The same actor or tool renamed every sentence so the prose looks varied.

| Pattern | Problem |
|---|---|
| scheduler / orchestrator / coordinator | One process, three names. The reader hunts for a second system. |
| app / platform / solution / offering | Marketing thesaurus. |
| users / individuals / folks / stakeholders | One group, four costumes. |

**Instead:** Pick the name the writer used first, or the most specific one. Reuse it.

## H11. Fake-Profound Kickers

A summary-recap or oracle last line that restates the piece in loftier words.

| Pattern | Problem |
|---|---|
| "And that changes everything." | Volume in place of a point. |
| "That's the whole game." | Pull-quote ending. |
| "Nothing else matters." | Fake stakes. |
| Closing paragraph that restates each section | The reader just read those sections. |

**Instead:** End on the last real point. If the draft already landed, stop.

## H12. Formatting Slop

Decoration that mimics structure.

| Pattern | Problem |
|---|---|
| Emoji headings | "🚀 Launch Day" is a poster, not a heading. |
| Decorative mid-sentence **bold** | Random stress. Emphasis belongs in the words. |
| Tiny header spam | A heading every two sentences. No room to think. |
| Horizontal rules as drama | Section breaks used as punchlines. |

**Instead:** Plain headings, few of them. Bold a phrase when you would italicize it in print. Then check whether the sentence works without the bold.
