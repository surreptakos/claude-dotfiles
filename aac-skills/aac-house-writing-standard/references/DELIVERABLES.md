# AAC-WR-001 - Rules 103-144. Email, Teams, letters, memos, reports, proposals and scopes, SOPs, contract and legal, technical

# Part XII - Email

## 100. Subject lines

Every business email should have a meaningful subject. The subject should identify the topic, project or customer when useful, and required action when applicable.

Clearbrook Northwind - Proposal Approval Needed

September Sales Report - Review Before Wednesday

Avoid subjects such as **Question, Update, FYI,** or **Important** without additional context. Update the subject when a long thread changes to a materially different topic.

## 101. Opening

Start with the purpose of the email. A greeting is optional depending on context and relationship. Do not routinely open with filler such as **I hope this email finds you well**.

## 102. Long emails

When an email is long, decision-oriented, or contains substantial background, put the bottom line first. The first paragraph should state what is happening, what is needed, who owns it, and by when. Then provide explanation.

## 103. Action ownership

Name the person who owns an action. When several people have separate responsibilities, assign each responsibility separately.

Chris: Complete the estimate.

Tina: Hold scheduling until the proposal is signed.

## 104. Deadlines

Use a specific deadline when timing matters. Prefer **Friday, September 11, at 3 p.m.** to **ASAP**. Use **ASAP** only when immediate action is genuinely required and the timing cannot be stated more accurately.

## 105. To and Cc

Put in **To** the people expected to act, directly responsible, or directly addressed. Put in **Cc** people who need visibility or a record but do not own the requested action. Do not use Cc as a substitute for telling someone what they need to do.

## 106. Attachments

When an attachment is necessary to the action, identify it. If several attachments exist, identify the important ones by name or purpose. Use filenames that allow the recipient to identify the document after saving it.

Before sending, check whether the attachment is a clean issue or a review copy. Remove comments and tracked changes from a clean issue; preserve them when the recipient is being asked to review those changes. A hidden-markup display is not a clean copy.

## 107. Email closing

Routine internal messages do not require a ceremonial closing. Formal external messages may use **Thank you, Regards,** or **Sincerely,** as appropriate. Do not use a closing that conflicts with the substance or tone of the message.

## 108. Email signature

Use the approved company signature format. At minimum include name, job title, company, and primary telephone number; include email or website when useful. Do not add personal quotations, inspirational sayings, unapproved images, decorative fonts, or excessive colors.

# Part XIII - Teams and business chat

## 109. General standard

Teams is less formal than email, but business-critical communication still requires clarity. A message assigning work should identify the person, action, and timing when relevant.

Chris, please send Mark the estimate before the 2 p.m. meeting.

## 110. Mentions

Use @mentions when a person needs to act, needs immediate attention, or is being added to a discussion where input is required. Do not @mention large groups for routine information.

## 111. Permanent records

Do not rely on Teams chat as the only record of a material customer commitment, contract decision, pricing approval, scope change, policy decision, safety issue, or project authorization. Record the final decision in the appropriate permanent system, document, ticket, CRM record, or email when required.

# Part XIV - Business letters

## 112. House letter format

Use full-block format. All elements begin at the left margin except items built into approved letterhead. Do not indent body paragraphs. Use a colon after the salutation and a comma after the complimentary closing.

## 113. Letter sequence

A formal letter should normally contain company letterhead, date, recipient name, recipient title, organization, street address, city/state/ZIP Code, salutation, optional subject line, body, complimentary closing, signature, typed name, title, enclosure or attachment notation when applicable, and copy notation when applicable.

## 114. Inside address

Use the recipient's preferred organization name and address. Spell out ordinary address terms in the inside address when space permits. Use USPS abbreviations where postal conventions call for them.

## 115. Salutation

Use **Dear Mr. Smith:**, **Dear Ms. Johnson:**, or the person's professional title. In an established first-name business relationship, **Dear Mark:** is acceptable. Avoid generic salutations when a specific person or role can reasonably be identified.

## 116. Subject line

A subject line may appear in the approved template before or after the salutation. Use a concise description. Do not use both **Subject:** and **Re:** for the same line.

## 117. Signature block

Leave adequate space for an actual or electronic signature. Follow approved company letterhead and signature standards.

Keep the closing, signature, typed name, and title together. On a continuation page, include at least two lines of body text above the closing, as required by Section 85.

# Part XV - Memoranda

## 118. Memo use

Use a memorandum when information should become part of a formal internal record. Use email or Teams for routine temporary coordination.

## 119. Memo heading

Use the guide words **DATE, TO, FROM, SUBJECT** in the approved memo template. Align the entries following the guide words. The subject should state the specific matter addressed.

## 120. Memo body

Use full-block paragraphs, no paragraph indentation, and the Word body spacing in Section 80. State the purpose in the first paragraph. Use headings when a memo exceeds about one page or addresses several distinct issues.

# Part XVI - Reports

## 121. Report structure

Use the sections appropriate to the report's purpose. Possible components include cover or title page, executive summary, table of contents, purpose or background, findings, analysis, recommendations, action items, appendices, and source notes. Do not add sections solely to make a report appear formal.

## 122. Executive summary

Use an executive summary when a decision-maker needs the conclusion without reading the entire report. State the principal finding, material evidence, recommended decision or action, and major risk or limitation. Do not use the executive summary merely to describe what the report contains.

## 123. Tables of contents

Use a table of contents for longer formal reports, manuals, policies, SOP collections, and documents where readers need to navigate among sections. As a house rule, consider a TOC once a document exceeds approximately five substantive pages. Use automatic heading-based generation in Word.

Keep table-of-contents entries synchronized with the document headings. Align page numbers at a right-hand tab stop with dot leaders. Update the entire table after edits and verify the listed page numbers against the issued document.

## 124. Data dates

Every recurring management report should state the reporting period or data-as-of date. Do not present stale data as current.

## 125. Findings and interpretation

Separate what the data says, why it happened, and what action should follow. Do not state an interpretation as though it were a measured fact.

# Part XVII - Proposals and scopes of work

## 126. Scope writing

Write scope items so the reader can determine who performs the work, what is furnished, what is installed or changed, the quantity, the location, and any relevant condition or assumption.

Preferred: Active Alarm will furnish and install two card readers at the rear double doors.

Avoid: Add readers at rear doors.

## 127. Quantities

State quantities where omission could create a scope dispute. Do not rely on plural nouns alone when the exact number matters.

## 128. Locations

Use specific locations. Avoid vague phrases such as **as needed** or **where required** unless another document or standard objectively defines the locations.

## 129. Work by other parties

Identify the party whenever known.

Preferred: The electrical contractor will provide a dedicated 120 V circuit at the control panel.

Avoid: 120 V by others.

Use **by others** only when the responsible party has not been determined or when an established contract format requires it.

## 130. Exclusions and assumptions

State exclusions and assumptions directly.

Price excludes 120 V power wiring.

Pricing assumes existing conduit is reusable.

Avoid vague catch-all language when a specific exclusion or assumption can be identified.

# Part XVIII - Procedures and SOPs

## 131. Standard SOP structure

Unless the controlled SOP system requires another structure, use: title, document number, version, effective date, owner, purpose, scope, responsibilities, required inputs or prerequisites, procedure, exceptions or escalation, records or system entries, related documents, and revision history.

## 132. Procedure steps

Use numbered steps. Write each primary step as an instruction and start with a verb whenever practical.

1. Open the customer record in Zoho CRM.

2. Confirm the site address.

3. Attach the signed proposal.

## 133. One action per step

A step may contain closely related actions, but do not bury several independent requirements in one paragraph. Use substeps when necessary.

## 134. Roles rather than names

Controlled procedures should assign recurring responsibilities to roles rather than individual employees. Use a person's name only when the document concerns that specific person rather than the continuing role.

## 135. Exceptions

State who can authorize an exception.

Preferred: The General Manager may approve an exception to this requirement in writing.

Avoid: Exceptions may be made when necessary.

## 136. Screenshots

Use screenshots only when visual information materially helps the instruction. Show only relevant screen content, avoid unnecessary personal or customer information, keep the image legible when printed, and use a short caption when the purpose is not obvious. Do not use a screenshot as a substitute for writing the required action.

# Part XIX - Contract and legal writing

## 137. Existing legal language

Do not revise approved contract language solely to make it conform to ordinary business-writing preferences. Legal meaning controls style. Contract forms should have designated owners and version control.

## 138. Defined terms

Capitalize a contractual term only when the document defines it as a term or established legal drafting requires the capitalization. Do not capitalize ordinary nouns merely to make them appear important.

## 139. Shall, must, and will

In company-authored noncontract documents, **must** expresses a requirement, **will** expresses a future action or commitment, and **may** expresses permission. In contracts, preserve approved legal drafting conventions. Do not change **shall** to **will** or **must**, or the reverse, without considering the legal effect.

## 140. Legal names

Use exact legal entity names in contracts, amendments, notices, releases, certificates, legal demands, and other documents where entity identity matters. Do not abbreviate a legal entity name before it has been properly established in the document.

# Part XX - Technical writing

## 141. Technical accuracy controls style

Do not sacrifice technical accuracy for ordinary prose conventions. Preserve manufacturer names, product names, part numbers, model numbers, serial numbers, device identifiers, drawing references, circuit numbers, door numbers, software versions, code sections, and customer identifiers exactly.

## 142. Technical units

Use accepted unit symbols in technical material. Maintain a space between the number and unit when standard technical practice requires it. Follow the applicable engineering or drawing standard when it requires another notation.

## 143. Model and part numbers

Do not insert punctuation, change capitalization, remove leading zeros, convert text to a number, or apply ordinary spelling rules to a model, serial, device, or part identifier. Copy identifiers from the authoritative source whenever practical.

## 144. Code references

When citing a code requirement, identify the source with enough precision for another person to verify it. When known and relevant, include the code or standard, edition, section, and applicable jurisdiction. Do not state that a code requires something unless the requirement has been confirmed.
