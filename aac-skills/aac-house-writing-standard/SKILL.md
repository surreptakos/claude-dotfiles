# AAC house writing standard

Condensed from "AAC-WR-001 House Writing and Document Standard v0.2" (Dan Gatsakos approved the content 9/9/26; full text in the Performance Review Audits project folder as .md and .docx). Where Gregg allows two treatments, this picks one. Rules marked "house" are AAC choices, not Gregg.

## Order of authority


Law or code; executed contract; AHJ requirement; manufacturer nomenclature; approved company template; this standard; The Gregg Reference Manual (11th ed.); Merriam-Webster for spelling. Never restyle contract language, quotations, model numbers, or a customer's own name.

Performance review documents and the coaching emails about them follow the AAC review standards (SEER, Sum-Ex, Core Message, third person, one example per item, minimal commas in review cells) and "AAC House Layout Standard (Gregg-based).md" first. Serial comma, headline case, "should," and e.g./i.e. rules below do not apply inside a review or its audit.

## Writing


- Main point first. An email asking for a decision names the decision, the owner, the deadline, and the reason in the first paragraph.
- Name the actor. "Mark will send the revised proposal," not "the proposal will be sent." Passive only when the actor is unknown or irrelevant, never to hide responsibility. Do not write "we" when one person owns the action.
- Match certainty to evidence: is/will for facts and commitments, should for recommendations, may/might for real uncertainty, "approximately" for estimates. Do not hedge by reflex.
- Must = required. Will = commitment. Should = preferred. May = permitted. Shall only in contracts.
- Plain words: use, before, after, start, because. Cut "please be advised," "it should be noted," "in order to," "at this time," "I hope this finds you well."
- One subject per paragraph, block style, blank line between paragraphs, no first-line indent. No paragraph per sentence without reason.
- Contractions fine in email, Teams, and routine internal writing. Not in contracts, policies, demand letters, certifications.
- Complete sentences in prose. Fragments only in headings, labels, tables, short action lists.

## Punctuation


- One space after end punctuation.
- Serial comma in all company prose (house). Exception: performance review cells.
- Comma before and/but/or/so joining two independent clauses; none when the conjunction joins words or phrases.
- Comma after an introductory clause; short phrases need none unless ambiguous.
- Nonessential information set off with commas ("which," with commas); restrictive without ("that," no commas).
- Semicolon between closely related independent clauses with no conjunction, or in a series with internal commas. When a period works, use the period.
- Colon only after a complete statement introducing a list, explanation, or quotation. Never right after a verb or preposition ("The items are: a, b, c" is wrong). Colon after a formal letter salutation.
- Hyphenate compound modifiers before a noun (60-month agreement, customer-provided equipment); not after the noun; never after an -ly adverb. Dictionary decides established compounds.
- En dash for ranges in formal documents (pages 4–8, 2025–2026, 8–10 a.m.). Never after "from" or "between": from 8 a.m. to 10 a.m. Hyphen acceptable in plain-text systems.
- No em dashes in company writing (house). Comma, colon, parentheses, or a new sentence.
- Parentheses for secondary detail only; never bury a condition or action in them.
- Avoid slashes in prose; no "and/or." Slashes fine in fractions, file paths, 24/7.
- Quotation marks for exact words only, never emphasis. Periods and commas inside the closing mark; colons and semicolons outside.
- Apostrophe for possession (the customer's system, the customers' systems, Chris's estimate). Never for plurals: POs, PDFs, 1990s.
- No exclamation points in formal writing; sparingly in internal chat; never doubled.
- Ellipsis only for omitted words in a quotation.

## Capitalization


- Proper names, official organization names, official document titles, formal codes (National Electrical Code, NFPA 72). Lowercase common nouns.
- Company: "Active Alarm Company, Inc." when the legal entity is required; "Active Alarm Company" or "Active Alarm" otherwise; "AAC" internally.
- Job titles capitalized only directly before a name (General Manager Daniel Gatsakos) or in org charts, signature blocks, directories, form labels. Lowercase after a name or standing alone.
- Formal department names capitalized (Operations); generic references lowercase (the sales team, our accounting function).
- Other organizations' names exactly as they style them (MOSCOT, GreenState, QuoteWerks).
- Generic system types lowercase: fire alarm system, access control system, video surveillance system, monitoring service. Product and platform names capitalized.
- Document titles and major section headings in headline case; table column heads, form prompts, UI labels, run-in headings, and informal email headings in sentence case. The review project uses sentence case throughout.
- Contract terms capitalized only when the document defines them.

## Numbers, dates, time, money


- Spell out one through ten; figures for 11 and up (Gregg, not the AP nine/10 rule).
- Related numbers in one sentence take one style; if any is over ten, all are figures ("8 doors and 14 doors").
- Spell out a number that starts a sentence, or recast.
- Indefinite numbers in words (hundreds of devices, a few thousand dollars).
- Figures always with units, symbols, and technical or contractual values: 5 feet, 120 V, 20 A, 2-hour test, 5-year agreement, 60-month term, $5, 39 percent.
- Dates in prose: September 9, 2026. No ordinal endings (never September 9th). Month-year without a comma: September 2026. Comma after the year mid-sentence. Numeric dates only in forms, tables, file names, and system fields; YYYY-MM-DD when sortable.
- Time: 8 a.m., 8:30 a.m., 3 p.m. Lowercase a.m./p.m. with periods, one space before them, no ":00" on the hour. Noon and midnight in words. Time zone on hard deadlines across zones.
- Time ranges: 8–10 a.m.; 11 a.m.–1 p.m.; or from 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. Never "from 8–10."
- Money: $5, $42.50, $1,250, $28,882. No ".00" on whole dollars unless a column mixes cents. $1.6 million for large rounded amounts.
- Percent: "39 percent" in narrative prose; "39%" in tables, dashboards, charts, financial schedules, and anything quoting a workbook. One form per document.
- Measurements: figures always; unit abbreviations in technical text and tables (5 ft, 8 in, 120 V); spelled in narrative. Do not convert dimensions an engineer or manufacturer supplied.
- Fractions in words when simple and nontechnical (one-half); figures with a leading zero for technical values (0.5 A).
- Ordinals spelled first through tenth; figures from 11th. No ordinal suffix on month-first dates.
- Phone: 847-555-1234 (hyphens, no parentheses).

## Abbreviations and spelling


- Do not abbreviate ordinary words to save space in prose. Abbreviations belong in tables, forms, drawings, and established terms.
- Spell out an unfamiliar term on first use, acronym in parentheses: authority having jurisdiction (AHJ). Skip the definition when every reader knows it (RMR, PO, NFPA). Customer-facing writing avoids internal acronyms.
- No periods in capital abbreviations: CEO, PDF, PO, RMR, GP, CRM.
- Keep the organization's own suffix styling; never add or drop the comma before Inc.
- States spelled out in prose (Lake Zurich, Illinois); USPS codes in addresses (Lake Zurich, IL 60047).
- U.S. spelling. email, website, internet, online. setup/set up, follow-up/follow up, login/log in, backup/back up (noun or adjective one word or hyphenated; verb two words).
- "and," not "&," except in official names and cramped table heads.
- "etc." sparingly, never "and etc.," never where the omitted items change a requirement or scope.
- Prefer "for example" and "that is" over e.g. and i.e. in prose; the abbreviations are acceptable in parentheses and technical writing when used correctly.

## Grammar


- Subject-verb agreement; every pronoun points to one obvious noun. Rewrite any "this," "that," "it," or "they" a reader could misassign.
- Singular they when gender is unknown or irrelevant (house choice, not Gregg).
- that = restrictive, which = supplemental with commas. who for people. whom only in formal writing; recast if it sounds stiff.
- Parallel construction in every list and series; action lists start with parallel verbs.

## Deliverable-specific rules

The rules above apply to every deliverable. Each deliverable type adds its own; read the named section before drafting, and every layout file before producing a Word document, table, or file name.

- Email, Teams message, letter, memo, report, proposal or scope, SOP, contract or technical text: the matching section of [`document-types.md`](document-types.md).
- Any Word document, report, or SOP: [`layout.md`](layout.md) in full (page setup, headings, lists, tables, file names).
- Any table, in any deliverable: the Tables section of [`layout.md`](layout.md).
- Any file handed over: the File names section of [`layout.md`](layout.md).

## Before sending


- Verify recipient, names, company names, dates, dollar amounts, quantities, percentages, addresses, deadlines, attachment references, identifiers, calculations, links, page numbers, version, signatures.
- Read for meaning: does the reader know why this was sent, what the action is, who owns it, by when, what is assumed, and what is fact versus estimate?
- Spellcheck assists; it does not decide terminology, names, code references, scope, or tone.
